[[Elections in the United States|Elections]] were held on November 3, 2026 to elect members of the [[United States Senate]] for the [[120th United States Congress|120th Congress]]. 33 [[Classes of United States senators#Class 2|Class 2 seats]] out of the 100 Senate seats were up for election, along with special elections in [[Florida]] and [[Ohio]]. The opposition [[Senate Democratic Caucus|Democratic Caucus]] led by [[Chuck Schumer]] defeated the [[Republican Party (United States)|Republican]] majority, led by [[John Thune]]. The Democratic Caucus is projected to have secured at least 55 seats—its largest post-election majority since the [[2012 United States Senate elections|2012 elections]].<ref>{{Cite news |url=https://www.nytimes.com/live/2026/11/04/us/midterm-election-results-democrats/aabb7671-9de1-569a-b3f6-001a182e469a |title=Democrats will hold at least 54 seats in the Senate in the next Congress{{nbsp}}... |last1=Reynolds |first1=Evan |last2=Vordick |first2=Jillian |work=[[The New York Times]] |date=November 4, 2026 |access-date=November 4, 2026}}</ref> Candidates in these elections were elected to six-year terms beginning in January 2027 and expiring in January 2033.

Senate seats are divided into [[Classes of United States senators|three classes]] whose terms are staggered, so that a different class is elected every two years. Class 2 senators were last elected in [[2020 United States Senate elections|2020]]. With the [[2024 Senate Republican Conference leadership election|election]] of John Thune as leader of the [[Senate Republican Conference|Republican Conference]], these were the first races since the [[2006 United States Senate elections|2006 elections]] in which the Republicans were not led by [[Mitch McConnell]]. The Senate Democratic Conference has been led by Chuck Schumer since 2017.

The Democrats have won 21 out of 35 contested seats, including 8 Republican-held seats, with Republicans having defended 13 seats. One seat ([[2026 United States Senate election in Alaska|Alaska]]) remains undecided {{asof|2026|11|12|lc=y|df=us}}. If Democrats win Alaska, they will have flipped seven seats in states that [[Donald Trump]] won by at least 11 points in [[2024 United States presidential election|the preceding presidential election]], the most such seats since [[1986 United States Senate elections|1986]].<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/12/politics/democrats-senate-wins-midterms |title=Here's how big Democrats won in the Senate in 2026 |publisher=CNN |last=Blake |first=Aaron |date=November 12, 2026 |accessdate=November 12, 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20261112235613/https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/12/politics/democrats-senate-wins-midterms |archive-date=November 12, 2026}}</ref> These Democratic flips would also represent the first time since the ratification of the [[Seventeenth Amendment to the United States Constitution|17th Amendment]] where a party won at least five seats in states that the opposing party won in the preceding ten presidential elections.<ref>{{cite AV media |date=November 11, 2026 |title=This GOP wipeout just keeps getting bigger by the day |type=YouTube video |language=English |publisher=CNN |url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S25JKr-YDYs |access-date=November 11, 2026}}</ref>

[[By-election|Special elections]] were held concurrently in [[2026 United States Senate special election in Ohio|Ohio]] and [[2026 United States Senate special election in Florida|Florida]] after the resignations of [[JD Vance]] and [[Marco Rubio]] to become [[Vice President of the United States|vice president]] and [[United States Secretary of State|secretary of state]], respectively. Against Democratic challengers, Republicans suffered a defeat in Ohio and won a narrow victory in Florida. The appointed incumbents in the seats—Republicans [[Jon Husted]] and [[Ashley Moody]]—were running to complete their terms until after the [[2028 United States Senate elections|2028 elections]].

Two incumbent senators—[[Bill Cassidy]] of [[Louisiana]] and [[John Cornyn]] of [[Texas]]—lost renomination in their respective primaries, while two nominees—[[Graham Platner]] (who dropped out) and [[Lindsey Graham]] (who died)—were replaced by a convention in [[2026 United States Senate election in Maine|Maine]] and a special primary in [[2026 United States Senate election in South Carolina|South Carolina]], respectively. In three states ([[2026 United States Senate election in Idaho|Idaho]], [[2026 United States Senate election in Nebraska|Nebraska]], and [[2026 United States Senate election in South Dakota|South Dakota]]), the Democratic nominee withdrew from the race, effectively clearing the field for an independent candidate.

==Partisan composition==
All 33 [[Classes of United States senators|Class 2]] Senate seats and two Class 3 seats were up for election in 2026. Class 2 currently consists of 20 Republicans and 13 Democrats.<ref>{{cite web |title=Class II - Senators Whose Terms of Service Expire in 2027 |url=https://www.senate.gov/senators/Class_II.htm |publisher=United States Senate |access-date=January 22, 2026}}</ref> Democrats defended two seats in states won by [[Donald Trump]] in [[2024 United States presidential election|2024]], in both cases by less than 3 percentage points: [[2026 United States Senate election in Michigan|Michigan]], where incumbent [[Gary Peters]] did not run for reelection; and Georgia, where the incumbent is [[Jon Ossoff]]. Michigan elected Democrat [[Elissa Slotkin]] to the Senate in [[2024 United States Senate election in Michigan|2024]] by 0.3 percentage points. Georgia did not have a Senate election in 2024, although in its most recent Senate election, in [[2022 United States Senate election in Georgia|2022]], Democrat [[Raphael Warnock]] won by three points in a runoff.<ref name="margin">{{Cite web |date=November 26, 2024 |title=2024 Presidential Elections By State Margin of Victory |url=https://www.foxnews.com/elections/2024/general-results/margin-of-victory |access-date=November 26, 2024 |website=Fox News Elections}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |title=Election Results |url=https://results.sos.ga.gov/results/public/Georgia/elections/2022DecGenRun |access-date=August 4, 2025 |publisher=Georgia Secretary of State}}</ref>

Five incumbent Democratic senators represent states won by [[Kamala Harris]] by single-digit margins in 2024: New Hampshire's [[Jeanne Shaheen]], Minnesota's [[Tina Smith]], Virginia's [[Mark Warner]], New Jersey's [[Cory Booker]], and New Mexico's [[Ben Ray Luján]].<ref name="margin" /> Shaheen and Smith did not run for reelection.<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/jeanne-shaheen-wont-run-re-election-opening-another-democratic-senate-rcna196015 |title=Jeanne Shaheen won't run for re-election, opening another Democratic Senate seat in 2026 |date=March 12, 2025 |publisher=NBC News}}</ref> [[Susan Collins]]'s seat in Maine was the only seat defended by a Republican in a state that Kamala Harris won in [[2024 United States presidential election in Maine|2024]].<ref>{{Cite web |url=https://www.politico.com/live-updates/2025/03/25/congress/susan-collins-polling-00246823 |title=Will Democrats finally get a shot at taking out Susan Collins? |date=March 25, 2025 |website=Politico}}</ref> Republican [[Thom Tillis]] of North Carolina chose not to run for reelection<ref name="politico carney">{{Cite web |author=Carney, Jordain |url=https://www.politico.com/news/2025/06/29/thom-tillis-retires-00432045 |title=Thom Tillis says he will retire following Trump attacks |date=June 29, 2025 |website=Politico}}</ref> in a state that Trump won by a single-digit margin in [[2024 United States presidential election in North Carolina|2024]].<ref name="margin" />

The 2026 Senate map was considered favorable to Republicans, even though Democrats defended 13 seats and Republicans 22. Most rating groups identified two Republican-held seats as highly competitive and three or four others as somewhat competitive. By contrast, Democrats needed to flip at least four seats to win a majority while defending two highly vulnerable seats and two others viewed as somewhat vulnerable.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Why Republicans start out as favorites in the 2026 Senate elections |url=https://abcnews.go.com/538/republicans-start-favorites-2026-senate-elections/story?id=116243572 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20241202212003/https://abcnews.go.com/538/republicans-start-favorites-2026-senate-elections/story?id=116243572 |url-status=dead |archive-date=December 2, 2024 |access-date=February 6, 2025 |publisher=ABC News |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Berman |first=Russell |date=May 12, 2025 |title=Retirement Is the New Resistance |url=https://www.theatlantic.com/politics/archive/2025/05/democrats-age-retirement-trump/682771/ |access-date=May 13, 2025 |website=The Atlantic |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date= April 24, 2025 |title=Senate Democratic retirements clear the way for a new generation: From the Politics Desk |url=https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/politics-news/senate-democratic-retirements-clear-way-new-generation-politics-desk-rcna202678 |access-date=May 13, 2025 |publisher=NBC News |language=en}}</ref> Pollsters found Democrats' chances to flip the Senate to be improving as the election approached due to highly competitive campaigns by nominees in states such as [[2026 United States Senate election in North Carolina|North Carolina]], [[2026 United States Senate special election in Ohio|Ohio]], [[2026 United States Senate election in Texas|Texas]], [[2026 United States Senate election in Kansas|Kansas]], and [[2026 United States Senate election in Alaska|Alaska]], and because of Trump's unpopularity amid the [[2026 Iran war]].<ref>{{Cite news |url=https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/07/11/here-are-races-watch-democrats-grow-more-bullish-retaking-senate/ |title=Democrats are growing bullish on retaking the Senate. ===Defeats===
Seven Republicans (including two appointees) sought either reelection or election (either to finish the term or a full six-year term), but lost either in the primary election or a special/general election. The last elected incumbent senator to lose renomination was [[Richard Lugar]] in [[2012 United States Senate election in Indiana|2012]],{{efn|name=PrimaryLoss|Republican senator [[Luther Strange]] ran to complete the term he was appointed to in [[2017 United States Senate special election in Alabama|2017]], but lost the primary to [[Roy Moore]], who narrowly lost the general election to Democratic nominee [[Doug Jones (politician)|Doug Jones]].}} and the last time multiple elected incumbent senators lost renomination in primaries was in [[2010 United States Senate elections|2010]].{{efn|Two elected incumbent senators lost renomination in primaries in 2010: [[Lisa Murkowski]] in [[2010 United States Senate election in Alaska|Alaska]] and [[Arlen Specter]] in [[2010 United States Senate Democratic primary election in Pennsylvania|Pennsylvania]]. After losing her primary, Murkowski ran a write-in campaign during the general election and won. In addition, [[Bob Bennett (politician)|Bob Bennett]] lost renomination at the state convention in [[2010 United States Senate election in Utah|Utah]].}} The last incumbent senator to place no better than third in a primary before Cassidy was [[Hattie Caraway]] in [[1944 United States Senate election in Arkansas|1944]].<ref name="SittingSenatorsPlaceThird">{{cite web|url=https://smartpolitics.lib.umn.edu/2026/02/09/how-often-do-sitting-us-senators-place-third-or-worse-in-primaries/|title=How Often Do Sitting US Senators Place Third (Or Worse) in Primaries?|publisher=Smart Politics|date=February 9, 2026|access-date=February 14, 2026}}</ref>{{efn|Republican senator [[Maurice J. Murphy Jr.]] ran to complete the term he was appointed to in [[1962 United States Senate special election in New Hampshire|1962]] but placed third in the primary.<ref name="SittingSenatorsPlaceThird" />}}

== Predictions ==

Multiple sites and political pundits have published predictions of competitive seats. These predictions have considered factors such as the strength of the [[incumbent]] (if the incumbent was running for reelection) and the other candidates, and the state's partisan lean, reflected in part by the state's [[Cook Partisan Voting Index]] rating. The predictions typically assign ratings to each seat, indicating the predicted advantage that a party had in winning that seat. Most election predictors use:
* "<u>tossup</u>": no advantage
* "<u>tilt</u>" (used by some predictors): advantage that is not quite as strong as "lean"
* "<u>lean</u>": slight advantage
* "<u>likely</u>": significant, but surmountable, advantage
* "<u>safe</u>" or "<u>solid</u>": near-certain chance of victory<!-- Colors and sorting:

In many cases, the incumbent has yet to say their intention of running for another term. If an incumbent intends to retire then place "<br />{{Small|(Retiring)}}" after the incumbent's name.

See [[Template:USRaceRating]] for how to apply rating.
