How the forecast works

Each race gets three estimates of the final margin, which are averaged with weights set by how accurate each kind has been in past elections:

  • Poll average. Every published poll of the actual nominees, weighted by recency (two-week half-life), sample size, likely-voter screens and the pollster's historical accuracy grade, with no single pollster allowed to dominate. Each pollster's typical lean relative to other polls of the same races is measured and removed; polls paid for by a campaign or party group are discounted by 2 points and given half weight.
  • Fundamentals. The seat's partisan lean (Cook PVI on the 2026 maps), incumbency, and the national generic-ballot average. For House seats it also uses each nominee's money raised from donors (FEC filings, self-funding excluded) and the incumbent's personal vote: how far they ran ahead of or behind their district in 2024, re-measured on the new lines. Fitted on 2018–2024 House results and FEC filings, tested by leaving each year out.
  • Expert and model ratings. The average of up to 11 published ratings (Cook, Inside Elections, Sabato, The Economist, Split Ticket, Decision Desk HQ, FiftyPlusOne, Fox, Silver Bulletin, RealClearPolitics, NBC), converted to an implied margin. These carry about 10–30% of the weight and capture local knowledge (candidate quality, money) the polls and fundamentals miss.

The forecast then simulates the election 40,000 times. Each simulation draws a nationwide polling miss (with fat tails for surprise years), a regional miss, a state miss and a race-specific miss, so races move together the way they do in real elections. The size of each layer was estimated by maximum likelihood from how 2010–2022 poll averages missed (table below). Win chances and seat counts are shares of those simulations.

The nationwide miss is not centred on zero. Polls overstated Democrats in 6 of the last 8 cycles, so every simulation assumes the polls overstate Democrats by — points, the average miss in the four midterms since 2010. Without that assumption the forecast would be more favorable to Democrats (see the sensitivity table).

Two things were tested and dropped: a full Bayesian model fitting every poll jointly (it trusted individual polls too much and missed by more than the simpler weighted average), and a slower poll decay (no gain).

Limits to keep in mind

  • Most House districts have no public polls; their forecasts rest on partisanship, incumbency and ratings.
  • District polls are often released by campaigns. They are discounted, but a few strong internal polls can still move a race.
  • Fundraising, candidate scandals and late news enter only through the published ratings.
  • Maine and Alaska use ranked-choice voting; final-round poll numbers were used where published.
  • This is a statistical estimate, not a prediction of certainty: a 70% favorite loses about three times in ten.
